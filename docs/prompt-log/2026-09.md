## The problem the lab is solving

A child with crouch gait wears a powered knee exoskeleton. For the motor to help at the right moment, the controller has to know where in the step the leg is right now: heel just struck, mid-stance, about to swing. That "where in the step" is **gait phase**, a number from 0% (heel strike) to 100% (the instant before the next heel strike). Roughly the first 60% is stance (foot on the ground) and the last 40% is swing.

Two small IMU sensors, one on the thigh and one on the shank, each give 6 numbers many times a second: acceleration in x, y, z and rotation rate in x, y, z. That is the 12 inputs. A neural network reads a short history of those 12 signals and outputs the phase.

## Why the output is two numbers, not one

Phase wraps around: 99% and 1% are physically next to each other but numerically far apart, so a network trained to output "percent" gets punished for a near-miss at the wrap. The fix is to put phase on a circle. The network outputs cos and sin of the phase (called gait_phase_x and gait_phase_y). You recover the percent with atan2(y, x). That is why the model has 2 outputs and why the "phase error" metric has to wrap into the range -50 to +50.

## The model Emily gave you

It is a **TCN**, a temporal convolutional network. Think of it as a stack of filters that slide along time, each looking at 5 consecutive samples. There are 5 stacked levels, each with 80 filters, and each level skips samples at twice the stride of the one below (dilation), so the top of the stack sees far back in time cheaply. The total lookback, the **receptive field**, works out to 249 samples. The checkpoint stores that number as 248, which is receptive field minus one, and yesterday's inspection confirmed the arithmetic against the file. It is causal, meaning it only looks backward, which is what a real-time controller needs.

It is small: about 296,000 parameters, 1.2 MB on disk. That matters for the plan, because a model this size may already run fast enough on a Pi, and you should measure before assuming inference is the slow part of the loop.

Two more facts about the checkpoint file itself:

- It is not a tar archive despite the name. It is PyTorch's own save format. It opens with `torch.load`, and it contains the recipe (layer sizes, kernel size, and per-channel normalisation values called center and scale) plus the weights.
- The recipe is useless without the kitchen. The class that turns the recipe into a runnable model is `TCN` inside a file called `biomechdata.py`, which lives in Emily's training repo and is not on your Mac. That is the single blocker.
